Learn to teach yoga, become a certified yoga instructor

Want to learn to teach yoga and become a certified yoga instructor? Start with what actually prepares you for a real class.

Will your yoga teacher training prepare you to teach a real class?

Completing a yoga teacher training and becoming a yoga teacher are two different things. If you want to learn to teach yoga, the certificate is only the start. You also need contact hours, supervised practice and feedback you can use. Keep reading for what those hours should include, the questions to ask before you enrol, and how a 200-hour foundation course can help you become a certified yoga instructor with confidence.

If you are thinking about becoming a yoga teacher, you have probably already started comparing programmes: prices, locations, teaching styles, schedules or studying online. Those questions are reasonable. There is another that deserves equal attention: will this training teach you how to practise yoga, or prepare you to teach it to others?

There is an important difference between the two.

Developing your own yoga practice is a personal journey. You learn postures, explore your breathing, improve your understanding of movement and discover how yoga affects you physically and mentally.

Teaching requires an additional set of skills.

You need to learn how to communicate clearly, demonstrate movements, guide students through a sequence, recognise different abilities, offer appropriate modifications and respond when something does not go according to plan. You also need to develop the confidence to stand in front of a group and take responsibility for the experience you are guiding.

These skills do not develop simply by practising yoga or studying its theory. They require dedicated learning, repeated practice, observation and feedback.

This is why I believe choosing your first yoga teacher training deserves careful consideration. The certificate matters, and so does the experience behind it. If your aim is to become a certified yoga instructor who can hold a room with care, those questions matter.

My experience: a 200-hour foundation training

I completed my 200-hour foundation yoga teacher training with Raili Maripuu and Katia Major at Thames Wellness Academy.

The programme consisted of 180 hours of in-studio contact time and 20 hours of self-paced study completed between modules.

This structure provides a useful starting point for understanding the different ways we learn during teacher training.

The in-studio hours offered a setting for learning alongside teachers and fellow students, while the self-paced hours provided time for independent study between modules.

Both forms of learning have a place in a comprehensive training programme. The important consideration is how they work together and whether the overall experience gives students the knowledge and practical skills they need to begin teaching.

Independent study allows you to revisit material, consolidate your understanding and work through information at your own pace. You can spend more time on subjects you find challenging and return to your notes whenever necessary.

Contact time offers different opportunities. Depending on how the sessions are structured, you can observe experienced teachers, ask questions, learn through demonstrations, practise with others and receive feedback in real time.

In a subject as practical as yoga, this interaction can be particularly valuable.

Understanding a movement intellectually is not quite the same as explaining it to another person. Knowing the names of postures does not automatically mean you can guide a class through them safely and confidently.

The challenge is learning to translate knowledge into teaching.

What do 200 hours actually mean?

When you see a yoga teacher training advertised as a 200-hour course, it is easy to assume that the number itself tells you what to expect.

The total number of hours is only one part of the picture.

You need to understand what those hours include, how the programme is delivered, how much time is dedicated to different subjects, and how your progress is assessed.

A comprehensive foundation training will typically cover several areas.

Yoga practice, postures and sequencing

You will learn about yoga postures, their variations, transitions between movements and the principles involved in creating a coherent practice.

Learning a posture for yourself is different from teaching it.

As a teacher, you need to consider how you introduce the movement, explain its purpose, offer accessible alternatives and help students understand what they are doing.

You also need to learn how postures fit together within a class. Sequencing involves more than choosing a collection of poses. It requires consideration of preparation, progression, pacing, balance and the needs of the people attending.

Anatomy and biomechanics

Understanding anatomy helps you make more informed decisions about movement, alignment and modifications. See also our Functional Anatomy CPD for teachers who want to go further after foundation training.

Students have different bodies, movement histories, physical capabilities and limitations. A position that feels comfortable for one person may not feel appropriate for another.

Your role is not to force everybody into the same shape. It is to develop the knowledge to guide students thoughtfully, recognise when an adjustment may be needed, and understand the limits of your own expertise.

Anatomy is therefore not simply a subject to memorise for an assessment. It is knowledge you draw upon when planning and teaching classes.

Teaching methodology and cueing

This is one of the areas aspiring teachers should examine particularly carefully.

Teaching involves communicating information in a way that students can understand and apply.

You must learn how to give clear instructions, demonstrate when necessary, choose appropriate language, manage the pace of a class and decide what information students need at each stage of a movement.

Too much information can overwhelm a student. Too little can leave them uncertain about what to do.

Developing effective cueing takes practice. You need opportunities to speak, demonstrate, observe how others respond and refine your instructions.

It is one thing to listen to an experienced teacher deliver a beautiful class. It is another to learn how to deliver one yourself.

Yoga philosophy and history

Yoga is more than a physical practice.

Depending on the tradition and the programme, teacher training may introduce students to yoga philosophy, historical contexts, ethical principles and texts such as the Yoga Sutras of Patanjali and the Bhagavad Gita.

These subjects can deepen your understanding of yoga and encourage reflection on the responsibilities that come with teaching.

They also invite you to consider what yoga means to you and how you wish to approach your role as a teacher.

Breathwork and meditation

Breathing practices, relaxation and meditation may form part of your training, depending on the style of yoga being taught. Related Journal reading: Breath work in professional teaching practice.

Learning these techniques for yourself is one aspect. Understanding how to introduce and guide them for other people is another.

You need to consider how you explain a technique, how you pace your instructions and how you respond if a student feels uncomfortable.

As with physical practice, teaching requires an understanding of both the method and the people you are guiding.

Practical teaching experience

Perhaps the most important question to ask is how much opportunity you will have to practise teaching.

Will you lead sections of a class? Will you practise giving instructions to fellow students? Will an experienced teacher observe you and provide constructive feedback? Will you have the opportunity to try again after receiving that feedback?

These experiences help bridge the gap between understanding the material and applying it.

You may know the sequence you want to teach, but standing in front of a group introduces a different set of challenges. You have to think about your language, timing, demonstrations, the flow of the class and the students in front of you, often simultaneously.

You cannot develop all these skills through theory alone.

A good training programme should give you meaningful opportunities to practise and develop them.

Contact hours: why the way you learn matters

The term contact hours refers to time spent in direct, structured interaction with a teacher or trainer. In a yoga teacher training, this may involve in-person sessions or, depending on the programme and its standards, live online instruction.

My own training included 180 hours of in-studio contact time and 20 hours of independent study.

When comparing programmes, I would encourage prospective students to look beyond the total number of hours and ask what contact time actually involves.

How much is dedicated to lectures and demonstrations? How much involves practical work? How much time will you spend teaching, observing others and receiving feedback?

These questions matter because not all contact hours serve the same purpose.

A day spent listening to lectures can be valuable, particularly when learning anatomy or philosophy. A practical session in which you practise teaching and receive individual feedback serves a different function.

Both can contribute to your development, and they are not interchangeable.

The same applies to independent study. Reading, watching demonstrations and completing assignments can strengthen your understanding, yet they do not necessarily replace the experience of guiding another person through a movement or leading a class.

The aim is not to dismiss one form of learning in favour of another. It is to understand what each offers and whether the combination is appropriate for the skills you need to develop.

Is online yoga teacher training enough?

Online learning has made yoga teacher training more accessible to people who might otherwise struggle with travel, scheduling, location or cost.

There are also different types of online training, and it is important not to treat them all as the same.

Some programmes are entirely self-paced, allowing students to study independently. Others include live online sessions, supervised teaching practice, mentoring and opportunities to receive feedback. Hybrid programmes combine online learning with in-person instruction.

These formats offer different learning experiences.

Self-paced study can be effective for theoretical subjects and independent reflection. Live sessions provide opportunities to interact with instructors and fellow students. In-person training can offer additional opportunities for direct observation, practical demonstrations and immediate feedback. Thames Wellness Academy delivers its Foundation Training on site in Berkshire and Reading.

Simply attending an in-person programme does not guarantee a high-quality education. Equally, an online programme is not necessarily inadequate.

The more useful question is not whether a course is online or in person, but how effectively it prepares you to teach.

For example, if you are learning how to give verbal instructions, you need opportunities to practise giving them. If you are studying sequencing, you need opportunities to create and teach sequences. If you are learning about modifications, you need to understand how to apply that knowledge to different students and situations.

Ask how the programme supports these learning outcomes.

If a course relies heavily on independent study, find out how practical teaching skills are developed, supervised and assessed. If it takes place in a studio, ask the same questions.

The delivery format matters, and what happens within that format matters more.

A certificate is a beginning, not the finish line

A 200-hour foundation training is an important milestone. It can provide the knowledge, experience and starting point for developing your teaching practice.

Completing a course and becoming a confident, capable teacher are not necessarily the same thing.

Teaching is a skill that continues to develop through experience.

Your first classes will teach you things that are difficult to anticipate during training. You will discover which instructions come naturally, which need refining, how you respond to different students and how you manage the many details involved in guiding a group.

You will continue learning from other teachers, your students, further education and your own practice. Academy CPD and workshops can support that next stage.

This is normal. You are not expected to know everything when you finish your foundation training.

What matters is that you understand what you have learned, recognise the limits of your knowledge and have developed a foundation from which to continue growing.

A certificate records the completion of a programme according to its requirements. It should not be treated as a guarantee of teaching competence in every situation.

When choosing your training, therefore, look for evidence that the programme takes practical teaching seriously and gives you a meaningful opportunity to develop your skills.

Questions to ask before enrolling

Before committing your time and money to a yoga teacher training, I would recommend asking the provider some direct questions. You can also contact Thames Wellness Academy, or read what are the entry requirements for 200h teacher training and how to become a yoga teacher.

About the structure

  • How many hours involve direct contact with instructors?
  • How many hours are self-paced or independent study?
  • What subjects are covered during each part of the programme?
  • How are the different learning components integrated?

About practical teaching

  • How much supervised teaching practice is included?
  • Will I practise teaching complete classes or shorter sections?
  • How will instructors assess my teaching skills?
  • Will I receive individual feedback?
  • Will I have opportunities to practise again after receiving feedback?

About anatomy and safety

  • How is anatomy taught, and how is understanding assessed?
  • How will I learn to offer modifications for different students?
  • What guidance is provided on physical adjustments and consent?
  • How does the programme address the limits of a yoga teacher's professional scope?

About the qualification

  • What certificate will I receive upon successful completion?
  • Is the programme recognised by the organisation or registry that matters to me?
  • What requirements apply if I want to teach in my intended setting?
  • What additional training, insurance or other requirements might I need before teaching professionally?

Do not hesitate to ask these questions before enrolling.

A well-informed decision begins with understanding what you are signing up for, not simply what the programme promises at the end.

Choosing the right training for you

There is no single training format that suits everyone.

Some people thrive in an intensive, in-person environment. Others benefit from a hybrid approach that allows them to study independently while attending practical sessions. For some, a live online programme with meaningful supervision may be the most realistic option.

Your circumstances matter, and so do your learning needs.

Think about how you learn best. Consider how much practical experience you want, what support you will need and whether the programme's teaching methods align with your expectations.

Look beyond promotional photographs, impressive descriptions and the headline number of hours.

Ask about the curriculum. Understand the assessment process. Find out who will teach you and how you will receive feedback. Most importantly, understand how the course prepares you to work with real students.

The aim is not to find the longest, most expensive or most prestigious programme.

It is to find a programme that provides a sound foundation for the kind of teacher you want to become.

Final thoughts: choose your training with intention

Starting a yoga teacher training is an exciting step. It can deepen your personal practice, introduce you to new perspectives and open the door to a different relationship with yoga.

Becoming a teacher also means taking responsibility for the people you guide.

That responsibility deserves thoughtful preparation.

My 200-hour foundation training at Thames Wellness Academy, with Raili Maripuu and Katia Major, consisted of 180 hours of in-studio contact time and 20 hours of self-paced study. This is the structure of the programme I experienced, and it has given me a concrete starting point for thinking about how yoga teachers learn.

For anyone considering their own training, I would encourage you to look carefully at the balance between independent learning, direct instruction and practical teaching experience.

Do not choose a programme based on the certificate alone. Understand what happens during the hours that lead to it.

Ask questions. Compare the curriculum. Find out how you will practise teaching and how your progress will be supported.

Remember that your foundation training is the beginning of your development, not the moment you stop learning.

Before you enrol, ask yourself one final question: when I complete this course, what will I actually be able to do that I cannot do today?

The answer may tell you more about the value of a training programme than the number of hours printed on its certificate.
